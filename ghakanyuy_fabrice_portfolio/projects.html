01 / PREDICTIVE ANALYTICSIn development

Hotel Booking Cancellation Prediction

Hotel cancellations create uncertainty for accommodation providers. A reservation may appear secure and then be cancelled, leaving rooms difficult to resell and making it harder to plan staffing, pricing and day-to-day operations. In this project, I use a hotel booking demand dataset to investigate cancellation behaviour and build an end-to-end predictive analytics workflow.

THE QUESTION I WANTED TO ANSWER

Can historical booking details and customer reservation patterns be used to predict which hotel bookings are more likely to be cancelled?

WHY I WORKED ON THIS PROJECT

I chose this problem because cancellations are a practical business challenge, not just a modelling exercise. I wanted to explore whether data could help hotels recognise cancellation risk earlier and make better-informed decisions about room availability and operational planning. I also wanted to practise the full data science process, including investigating data quality before training a model.

Problem & approach

The project focuses on understanding cancellation behaviour, assessing data quality and preparing a reproducible modelling pipeline. The initial dataset contains 119,390 records. A duplicate investigation identified 40,165 rows belonging to duplicate groups, including 31,994 additional copies; removing exact duplicates leaves 87,396 records.

Workflow

  • Inspect dataset structure, target distribution and data quality.
  • Investigate exact duplicates and groups with conflicting cancellation labels.
  • Clean and prepare data, then engineer predictive features.
  • Train and compare classification models using appropriate evaluation metrics.
  • Explain model behaviour and present findings through a Streamlit application.
Tools

Python · Pandas · NumPy · Scikit-learn · Streamlit

Key focus

Data quality · Classification · Feature engineering · Model evaluation

Current milestone

Duplicate investigation and data preparation.

02 / INTERACTIVE DATA ANALYTICSWorking application

AI Data Analysis Agent

The AI Data Analysis Agent is an interactive Streamlit application that helps users explore a dataset, understand its structure and move towards useful analytical questions. It combines automated data profiling and summary calculations with natural-language assistance, using a retail sales dataset as the working example.

THE QUESTION I WANTED TO ANSWER

How can I make the first stages of data analysis quicker and more accessible by allowing users to explore a dataset and ask questions about its contents in a more natural way?

WHY I WORKED ON THIS PROJECT

I wanted to build something practical that connects programming, data analysis and AI in one usable application. Analysts often spend time understanding unfamiliar data before they can investigate the business problem. This project gave me an opportunity to explore how automated profiling and conversational assistance could support that early exploration, while keeping the underlying calculations visible and checkable.

What it does

The application allows a user to work with a dataset, inspect its structure and generate useful analytical summaries. The project uses a retail sales dataset with 604 records and 17 columns. The initial data audit found four duplicate rows.

Example analysis

  • Total recorded revenue: £907,228.49.
  • Total recorded profit: £246,708.95.
  • Average revenue per record: £1,512.05.
  • Laptop was the highest-revenue product in the analysed dataset.
  • Online sales contributed £513,689.97 in revenue.

Learning

The project explores how automated summaries can help users move from raw tables to questions about products, sales channels, regions and profitability.

Tools

Python · Pandas · Streamlit · OpenAI API

Dataset

Retail sales analysis dataset · 604 rows · 17 columns

Focus

Data profiling · Business analysis · Interactive reporting

03 / FINANCIAL RISK ANALYTICSIn development

Credit Risk Intelligence Platform

This project explores whether information about customers’ credit accounts and repayment behaviour can help assess the risk of future credit card default. Using the UCI Default of Credit Card Clients dataset, I am developing a classification workflow to examine the relationship between repayment history, balances, payments and the recorded default outcome.

THE QUESTION I WANTED TO ANSWER

Can customer repayment history and credit account information be used to identify patterns associated with the likelihood of credit card payment default?

WHY I WORKED ON THIS PROJECT

My interest in finance and data science led me to choose a problem where analytical methods can inform risk assessment. I wanted to understand how classification models can be applied to credit-risk data, how an imbalanced target affects evaluation, and why measures such as precision, recall and F1-score matter when assessing model performance. The project is also an opportunity to build a practical risk-analysis application.

Dataset & objective

The project uses the UCI Default of Credit Card Clients dataset, containing 30,000 customer records and 24 variables. The target variable records 6,636 defaults (22.12%) and 23,364 non-defaults.

Planned workflow

  • Audit data quality, duplicates and target imbalance.
  • Explore credit limits, repayment status, bill amounts and payment amounts.
  • Prepare features and establish a baseline classification model.
  • Compare models using precision, recall, F1-score and ROC-AUC.
  • Develop an interactive Streamlit interface to explore risk predictions and model explanations.
Tools

Python · Pandas · Scikit-learn · Streamlit

Dataset

UCI Default of Credit Card Clients · 30,000 rows · 24 variables

Focus

Credit risk · Classification · Explainability

04 / MSc RESEARCHAcademic research

Semi-Supervised Ensemble Clustering for Financial Fraud Analysis

This MSc research project investigates whether semi-supervised clustering and ensemble methods can help identify structure in highly imbalanced financial fraud data. It compares several clustering algorithms and an ensemble consensus approach, using pairwise constraints as limited supervision and evaluating the results with clustering and fraud-detection measures.

THE QUESTION I WANTED TO ANSWER

To what extent can semi-supervised ensemble clustering methods use limited pairwise guidance to produce meaningful clusters in highly imbalanced financial fraud data, and how do their results compare with individual clustering methods?

WHY I WORKED ON THIS PROJECT

I chose this topic to explore the challenge of detecting fraud when fraudulent transactions are rare compared with legitimate activity. I was interested in whether combining clustering methods and incorporating a small amount of supervision could provide useful structure without relying entirely on labelled examples. The research also allowed me to examine the limitations of different evaluation measures and understand why high recall alone does not necessarily mean a model is effective at identifying fraud.

Methods investigated

The implementation compared K-Means, DBSCAN, Spectral Clustering, Agglomerative Clustering, COP-KMeans and an ensemble consensus approach. Pairwise constraints were used as supervision signals, and performance was examined using clustering and fraud-detection measures.

Evaluation and reflection

The evaluated ensemble results included ARI of -0.0009, NMI of 0.0001, silhouette score of 0.1647, precision of 0.0016, recall of 1.0 and F1-score of 0.0032. Constraint satisfaction accuracy was 0.8425.

These results show why accuracy alone is not enough for a severely imbalanced fraud problem. Although recall was high, precision and F1-score were very low, indicating that the approach generated many false positives. The project therefore highlights the importance of class imbalance, evaluation design and careful interpretation.

Tools

Python · Scikit-learn · NumPy · R

Methods

Clustering · Ensemble consensus · Pairwise constraints

Metrics

ARI · NMI · Silhouette · Precision · Recall · F1

05 / PROJECT LEADERSHIP & SOCIAL IMPACTAward-supported initiative

One Device, Multiple Impact

One Device, Multiple Impact was a digital inclusion initiative focused on supporting access to devices and digital learning for young people and communities affected by displacement. The work brought together a distributed group of ambassadors across Nigeria and Cameroon to support digital skills training and community impact.

THE QUESTION THAT GUIDED THE INITIATIVE

How can a coordinated network of volunteers and access to digital devices help more young people develop practical digital skills and participate in learning opportunities?

WHY I WORKED ON THIS PROJECT

I was motivated by the gap between the opportunities digital skills can create and the barriers some young people face in accessing devices and training. I wanted to contribute to an initiative that translated that concern into practical activity. My involvement also gave me the opportunity to develop my leadership and project coordination experience by working with ambassadors, supporting delivery and helping the initiative secure funding.

My contribution

  • Contributed to the design and development of the initiative.
  • Coordinated 40 ambassadors across Nigeria and Cameroon.
  • Supported digital skills training delivered to more than 200 young people.
  • Helped organise activities, communication and progress monitoring.
  • Supported the project in securing £2,500 through the Aberystwyth University Enterprise Award.

This experience strengthened my project coordination, communication, problem-solving and stakeholder engagement skills.

Organisation
